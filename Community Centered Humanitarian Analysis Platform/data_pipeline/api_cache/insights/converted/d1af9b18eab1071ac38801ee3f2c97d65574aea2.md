## 1. Executive Summary

More than two years of escalating conflict in Sudan has pushed the country into alarming levels of food insecurity and critical loss of livelihoods. In response to the situation, CORE (Community Organized Relief Effort) and Sudanese Development Call Organization (NIDAA) implemented Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance (MPCA) in three localities of Khartoum State: Karari, and Omdurman, Sharganeel. The program, implemented in collaboration with the Cash Consortium of Sudan (CCS), aimed to increase households' capacity to meet essential needs in a dignified manner, support market functionality to serve people through the influx of MPCA, and mitigate the imminent protection risks faced amid the ongoing conflict.

Between July 2024 and June 2025, CORE and NIDAA reached 1,231 conflict-affected households classified as IPC 4 and 5 via bank transfer. The MPCA transfer value was aligned with the Cash Working Group (CWG) agreed Minimum Expenditure Basket (MEB) amount of SDG 280,000 (approximately US$108.50) per household per month, distributed in local currency. Assistance was provided in one tranche, totaling SDG 840,000 (US$325.50). While the majority of households required a proxy recipient to receive assistance, all respondents confirmed receiving the full transfer value.

Post-distribution monitoring findings demonstrated the following results:

- Food Security: Households experienced improvements in food security outcomes as the share of households with poor food consumption decreased by 73%.
- Basic Needs: MPCA enhanced households' capacity to meet basic needs, and on average, households allocated two-thirds of the assistance to food, medical expenses, debt repayment, and education.
- Livelihood Coping Strategies: MPCA reduced households' reliance on several negative coping mechanisms; however, increased reliance on social networks and migration was observed, a trend that warrants further analysis.
- Well-being: Households reported improved well-being outcomes, most notably through reduced stress.

The program demonstrates that scaling up MPCA remains a critical strategy to save lives, preserve dignity, and strengthen household resilience across Sudan. To enhance future program models, future interventions should:

- Promote equitable partnerships with local actors to advance localization and ensure sustainability.
- Strengthen linkages between MPCA and livelihoods programming to support pathways toward self-reliance.
- Supporting recipients to open bank accounts, promoting financial inclusion and reducing reliance on proxies.
- Integrate gender and protection mainstreaming to mitigate any unintended consequences and promote social inclusion and gender equality.

## 2. Introduction and Operational Context

Since April 2023, Sudan has spiraled into an unprecedented humanitarian crisis. More than two years of escalating conflict has pushed the country into alarming levels of food insecurity. According to the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) system, which ranks food insecurity from Phase 1 (none) to Phase 5 (famine conditions), half of the Sudanese population (approximately 24.6 million people) are experiencing acute food insecurity (IPC Phase 3 or above). This represents an increase of 3.5 million people compared to the previous analysis in October 2024 and includes five areas where famine has been confirmed, as well as five more at imminent risk (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification [IPC], 2025). The situation is especially severe for female-headed households which experience food insecurity at twice the rate of male-headed households (United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs [OCHA], 2024). In Khartoum State, long the epicenter of the conflict, 75% of the population face high levels of food insecurity (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification [IPC], 2025).

Severe protection violations related to the conflict have triggered mass displacement across the country. The latest figures estimate more than 13 million people have been forced to flee their homes, either within or across borders, making this displacement crisis globally (International Organization for Migration [IOM], 2025). For the first time since the conflict began, the number of internally displaced persons (IDPs) has begun to decline. This recent shift is due to an influx of returnees, many of whom had been displaced out of Khartoum State. Most returnees have resettled in Omdurman-Karari locality.

The impact of the conflict extends beyond food insecurity and displacement. It has caused extensive damage including the destruction of local infrastructure, collapse of essential services, and loss of livelihoods, leaving millions without access to basic needs, deepening economic hardship, and exacerbating a severe public health crisis. As a result, the extreme poverty rate is expected to more than double from 33% in 2022 to 71% of the population in 2025, according to the World Bank.

In response, Community Organized Relief Effort (CORE) and its national partner. Sudanese Development Call Organization (NIDAA) implemented a Multi-Purpose Cash Assistance (MPCA) program targeting 1,231 hard-to-reach conflict-affected households in Karari, Omdurman, and Sharganeel in Khartoum State. The program, implemented in collaboration with the Cash Consortium of Sudan (CCS), demonstrated measurable improvements in food security, reduced reliance on negative coping strategies, enhanced household well-being, and strengthened capacity to meet basic needs. This learning brief presents key findings from 290 Post Distribution Monitoring surveys (32% female) and outlines lessons learned and recommendations to inform future at-scale MPCA responses in Sudan's evolving humanitarian context.

## Delivery Mechanism

MPCA was delivered in partnership with RedRose and Cashi, and distributed via bank transfers, specifically Bankak. To maximize accountability, efficiency and safety for staff and recipients, CORE and NIDAA adopted a fully digital transfer system during program design, drawing on lessons learned from a previous selffunded hybrid (physical and digital) MPCA distribution in Khartoum State. During implementation, the Humanitarian Aid Commission (HAC) announced that all humanitarian cash transfers should be limited to digital distribution; having already established this approach, CORE was able to proceed without delays. Close coordination with the Community Committees helped ensure distributions were conducted safely for both recipients and project staff despite the volatile security situation.

## Targeting, registration, verification, enrolment and proxies

In alignment with the CCS members, CORE and NIDAA coordinated with HAC to adopt a blanket targeting approach in IPC Phase4 areas of Khartoum State, given the prevailing security constraints at the time of implementation. NIDAA led community coordination with existing Community Committees and local leaders to ensure that households in these hard-to-reach areas were appropriately identified for assistance. All households on the Community Committee lists were first registered in hardcopy and subsequently digitized in the CCS CommCare system and deduplicated. For households without access to a bank account, proxy recipients were coordinated during registration by NIDAA staff and Community Committees. The majority of households (59%) required a proxy recipient arrangement. In these cases delegation forms were completed and signed by both the intended recipient and the proxy recipient. The Community Committees played a critical in the registration process, providing localized security analysis and facilitating safe access for staff and participants throughout registration.

## Referrals

CORE engaged with the CCS and Community Committees to establish and activate referral pathways for vulnerable individuals identified during registration and distribution. While the CCS referral system was being operationalized, CORE and NIDAA facilitated referrals through existing projects for health-related needs and critical pregnancy cases. All referrals involved women and children and originated from Shargalneel, an area under siege at the time, where access to basic services was extremely limited.

Despite these efforts, referral activities faced significant constraints, as most services in the area were nonoperational or severely limited. Escalating hostilities and ongoing clashes within intervention areas further restricted safe access to complementary services. In addition, the absence of a standardized referral system and designated focal points among partners created coordination challenges. In response, CORE and NIDAA established ad hoc referral mechanisms to ensure that urgent cases received at least minimal assistance.

## Community Feedback and Complaint Mechanisms (CFMs)

Various Community Feedback and Complaint Mechanisms (CFMs) were deployed during the program, including WhatsApp numbers for designated CORE and NIDAA staff, designated CORE and NIDAA protection focal points, and the Community Committees. Community Committees played an active role in providing regular feedback through a dedicated focal point for recipients.

## 4. Methodology, Data, and Limitations

## PDM Design and Sampling Strategy

Before MPCA distribution, CORE collected demographic data for all 1,231 cash recipients using CommCARE. CORE also conducted a baseline analysis with a representative random sample of 295 individuals. To measure the longitudinal impact of MPCA, a PDM survey was conducted. Data was collected using a structured questionnaire administered through KoboToolbox. The survey included mostly close-ended questions. The unit of analysis was household, and the survey covered seven components: (1) Demographic Information; (2) MPCA Expenditure; (3) MPCA Effectiveness; (4) Food Consumption Score (FCS); (5) Livelihood Coping Strategies; (6) Well-being; and (7) Accountability.

The data collection process was conducted over a seven-day period between the end of May and the beginning of June 2025, using a simple random sampling method was implemented. The sample size was estimated based on research standards. A total of 294 PDM surveys were collected. Following the data cleaning to remove incomplete submissions, a final representative sample of 290 valid responses was retained, representing 24% of all MPCA recipients. Before data collection, CORE staff and local partner NIDAA, in coordination with local authorities and Community Committees, conducted a community meeting with participants to

<!-- image -->

explain the purpose of the survey, how the data would be used, and their right to withdraw at any point. A total of seven enumerators (three female) were hired and received in-person training. CORE and NIDAA prioritized gender parity to ensure female interviewees felt comfortable completing the survey.

## Demographic Characteristics

The average respondent was female (68%) and 50 years old, with ages ranging from 18 to 84. At baseline, 12% were living with a disability. The majority of MPCA recipients (85%) were married. and 17% reported living in a polyamorous household. The typical household consisted of four members, which is smaller than the national pre-conflict average of six people per household. This result is likely due to the effects of mass displacement, as all respondents self-identified as IDPs. Almost one in 10 households included a pregnant or lactating woman.

## Limitations

Both a PDM survey and endline assessment were planned, but due to changes in funding allocations, it was not feasible to complete the endline. The PDM data collection process covered only participants from Omdurman due to security concerns, which limits the analysis of the report and the generalizability of findings to other affected areas of Khartoum State where the program provided MPCA. In addition, the return of displaced participants, many of whom were temporarily hosted in CORE's operational areas, limited the ability to conduct house-to-house interviews with some MPCA participants. Despite these limitations, the data provides insights into the outcomes of conflict-affected households reached through the MPCA program and provides insights to inform future programming.

## Nutrition: Food Consumption Score (FCS)

The FCS measures dietary quality and the frequency of food group consumption over a seven-day reference period. According to WFP's FCS classification, households were categorized into three groups: poor FCS (0-21), borderline FCS (21.5-35), or acceptable FCS (more than 35). Both baseline and PDM data were captured using this approach. The results suggest that MPCA contributed to improved household food security outcomes 2 . Figure 2 shows that the distribution of the FCS at PDM shifted to the right, as compared to baseline. This shift suggests an overall increase in average scores for the majority of participants. Following receipt of MPCA, most households scored above 35, which is the threshold for an acceptable food consumption level. The PDM distribution curve also appears wider than the baseline curve, suggesting greater variability in food security outcomes, as some households achieved scores between 60 and 75, a range that was uncommon at baseline.

## No Negative Coping Strategies

The proportion of households that did not need to rely on any negative coping strategies remained unchanged at 6% at both baseline and PDM. This result suggests ongoing vulnerability in households' ability to manage shocks. A gender comparison shows that all female-headed households reported using at least one coping strategy, compared to 7% of male-headed households who reported not relying on any. No statistically significant differences were observed between younger- and older-headed households.

## Stress strategies

As shown in Figure 5, the percentage of households reporting reliance on stress strategies declined fourfold at PDM. Disaggregated results after the intervention show there was no statistically significant difference in the use of stress coping strategies between male- and female-headed households, nor between younger- and older-headed households. At the individual level, reliance on three out of the four coping strategies decreased: purchasing food on credit or borrowing food (-43.35%), borrowing cash (-54%), spending savings (-168.35%). There was, however, an increase in the share of households that sold household assets or goods (+21.07%).

## Crisis strategies

The percentage of households reporting reliance on crisis coping strategies decreased twofold by PDM (see Figure 5). Disaggregated results show that female-headed households were 1.5 times more likely to rely on crisis strategies than male head of households. When looking at the analysis results by the individual coping strategies, there were a decrease in two out of the three strategies: withdrawing children from school 4 (-47.87%) and selling productive assets or means of transport (-63.29%).

There was an increase in households reporting reduced expenditure on essential non-food items (+19.11%). The concurrent increase in households selling non-productive assets/goods and the decrease in the sale of productive assets may have mitigated the need to sell productive assets. However, given that the level of needs remains high, households may have resorted to selling readily tradable household goods, in order to meet immediate consumption requirements.

## Emergency strategies

In contrast, reliance on emergency coping strategies doubled at PDM (see Figure 5). Disaggregated results show that older-headed households were 2.5 times more likely than younger-headed households to adopt these strategies after the intervention. The increase was driven by an increase of the share of households who relied on direct support from friends or family (+137.17%) and those reporting at least one household member migrated (+43.61%), the former likely explained by the overall context of increased forced displacement. The rise in household-level emergency strategies may appear counterintuitive given the decline in stress and crisis strategies.

A potential explanation is that the MPCA may have alleviated the need to adopt moderate coping mechanisms. Nonetheless, MPCA alone may have been insufficient to offset the effects of structural shocks such as hyperinflation and prolonged conflict. It is also possible that over time, households transitioned from less severe coping behaviors (e.g., spending savings that were fully depleted) to more severe strategies, such as relying on migration 5 or support from their networks. In addition, the increased reliance on families and friends may also suggest a strengthening of informal support systems and social networks, which could indicate distress but also community-level resilience.

## Well-being

Well-being was measured using an index 6 of four dimensions: (i) ability of the household to meet basic

needs, (ii) improved living conditions, (iii) reduced financial burden, and (iv) reduced feelings of stress 7 . Figure 6 shows that MPCA contributed to short-term improvements in participants' well-being outcomes. Close to 80% of participants scored in the high category on the well-being index after receiving MPCA. The results were higher for female participants (92.41%) and adults aged 65 and above (93.1%). The four dimensions are discussed in more detail below.

## Reduced feelings of stress

Two-thirds of MPCA recipients (66%) reported that MPCA enabled them to significantly reduce their feeling of stress. Although women and older adults also reported improvements in this dimension after PDM, the differences between men and women, and older and younger adults, were not statistically significant.

## MPCA Expenditure and Decision-Making

Spending patterns suggest that households used MPCA to meet their immediate basic needs. On average, participants allocated two-thirds of their MPCA to four expenditure categories: food and/or groceries, medical expenses, debt repayment, and education (see Figure 7). The results are consistent with the improvements observed in food security outcomes (Section 4.1). Expenditure patterns were, on average, similar across age and sex. The only exceptions were business investments and childcare expenses. Younger participants invested more in businesses activities (US$20.56) than older participants9 (US$8.58), whereas female recipients spent more (US$11.12) on childcare than male recipients (US$4.27). While most recipients were able to purchase all the items they needed, 30% reported that they could not buy certain food items (e.g., milk, fruits), medications, and furnishings. Responses regarding decision-making patterns varied: 87% of respondents reported that they themselves made decisions, while the remaining 13% reported decisions being led by other family members, most often their partners. The results were consistent by sex. For most households (93%), MPCA did not change existing decision-making dynamics. However, among those who reported changes (7%), there was an increase in joint decision-making between men and women.

## Modality, Transfer Value, Timing, and Frequency

## Response Modality and Delivery Mechanism

At baseline, all program participants indicated that bank transfer was their preferred delivery mechanism. At PDM, the preference for bank transfers remained high (96.94%). A small share of respondents preferred other modalities: 2.04% favored multi-modal support combining cash and in-kind assistance, and 1.02% preferred electronic transfers through the Cashi Wallet network.

Despite the preference for bank transfers, many participants did not have their own active bank accounts. Most respondents (59%) reported receiving assistance through authorized and designated proxies' accounts after they signed a delegation letter-proxies were most often members of their immediate or extended families as well as neighbors-while the remaining 41% received assistance directly into their own accounts. All participants that used proxy accounts reported receiving MPCA and, overall, experienced no major issues. The majority reported being satisfied with the process (97%), maintaining smooth communication with their delegated proxy recipient (93%), and receiving MPCA on the same day as communicated by CORE and NIDAA 10 . Despite the successes of proxy transfers, the high reliance on proxies underscores the need for support to open bank accounts. The civil registration system has rendered many program participants unable to renew their identification documents (IDs), thereby preventing them from having valid IDs to open bank accounts.

## Transfer Value, Timing and Frequency

All respondents confirmed receiving the full transfer value of SDG 840,000 (US$325.50). Information about the transfer was communicated primarily during community meetings (45.19%) and through local representatives (31.85%). 90% of respondents expressed that the transfer amount was equal to what the program team had communicated, while the remaining 10% reported that they were not informed in advance. The majority (93%) reported that they received MPCA on the expected day, and the remaining 7% were unsure. Ninety-four percent (94%) of respondents preferred to receive MPCA as a single tranche, as defined in the program design. Only 5% preferred multiple tranches 11 , and 1% reported no preference. Participants who preferred a single tranche explained that phased disbursement would reduce their ability to address urgent needs and risk additional value loss due to currency depreciation and high inflation.

## Community Feedback Mechanisms (CFMs)

The functionality and reach of CFMs were constrained by widespread internet and mobile network disruptions, which restricted the use of WhatsApp, e-mail, and hotlines. In addition, insecurity and heightened conflict reduced opportunities for in-person engagement with protection focal points and Community Committees. When asked about preferred channels for future feedback and complaints, respondents preferred a hotline (80.67%) and meeting with local representatives (9.66%) 12 .

## Respect, Needs Covered, Targeting, and Satisfaction

PDM respondents were asked about their perceptions of respect, how well their needs were covered, the targeting approach, and overall satisfaction. These are discussed below.

Respect: Ninety-eight percent (98%) of respondents reported being treated with respect by CORE and NIDAA staff during the full program cycle, including during assistance delivery. The remaining 2% did not elaborate further on the reasons.

Needs covered: Ninety percent (90%) felt their needs were considered. Among the 10% who disagreed, respondents expressed dissatisfaction with the transfer value, which they felt was insufficient due to rising prices and difficulties affording medicines. Disaggregated analysis indicated that female and older respondents were more likely to feel that their needs were not considered. Nonetheless, the differences were not statistically significant.

Targeting: Perceptions on targeting were mixed; while 40% of respondents were satisfied with the selection process, another 40% reported knowing someone in need of MPCA who was not included in the program. It was unclear from the phrasing of the question whether responses referred to individuals excluded from assistance within the target geography or not reached by humanitarian assistance in general.

Satisfaction: Nearly all participants reported being 'very satisfied' or 'satisfied' with the overall program, with only two individuals expressing neutral opinions. Suggestions for improvement included increasing the transfer value, adjusting the transfer amounts based on household size, providing small business grants alongside MPCA, and expanding assistance coverage to reach more families.

## Transparency, Fairness and Safeguarding

To ensure issues related to transparency, fairness and safeguarding within MPCA could be identified and addressed, participants were asked during the PDM whether anyone had requested a fee, gift, tip, service, or favor in exchange for assistance. ## 7. Conclusions

As Sudan continues to be devastated by the ongoing conflict, MPCA remains essential to saving lives, preventing the collapse of market systems, reducing the reliance of vulnerable households on risky coping mechanisms, and protecting household assets. This pervasive humanitarian crisis is set against the backdrop of a protracted economic crisis, with the revised 2025 Humanitarian Response Plan (HRP) reporting that about 25 million (about 50% of the population) need humanitarian assistance and protection as the devasting conflict threatens to consume the entire country.

Consistent linkages between basic needs programming, sustainable livelihoods, income-generating activities, and recovery programming are more important than ever to ensure self-reliance and reduce dependence on humanitarian assistance. CORE and NIDAA's success reaching the hardest-to-reach communities in Khartoum, in the epicenter of the conflict, was underpinned by an equitable partnership that leveraged and strengthened the capacity of local organizations in assessments, coordination with key stakeholders (including FSPs), and harmonization efforts. CORE and NIDAA's partnership serves as a blueprint for other INGOs collaborating with local organizations.

The scale up of MPCA remains an essential strategy to save lives across Sudan. Moving forward, to enhance the program model, future interventions should: strengthen linkages between MPCA and livelihoods programming to support self-reliance; support recipients in opening bank accounts to promote financial inclusion and reduce reliance on proxies; and ensure ongoing gender and protection mainstreaming to mitigate unintended consequences and promote social inclusion and gender equality.
